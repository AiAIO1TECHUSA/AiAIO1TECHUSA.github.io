AuthorPatricio Lucero, Paralegal
RecipientSupervising Attorney

SubjectEighth Amendment Issues of Law Concerning Mental Illness and Capital Sentencing
Statement of Facts

The defendant is a young mother who, in the months preceding the offense, experienced severe postpartum depression and symptoms consistent with postpartum psychosis. During this period, she repeatedly told neighbors, friends, and acquaintances that she was schizophrenic, that she heard voices, and that she feared she was losing control of her thoughts. Witnesses reported that she openly sought help, expressed confusion about her mental state, and stated that she did not feel safe being alone with her children.

Her mental deterioration escalated into multiple suicide attempts. During these episodes, she caused physical harm to her children while attempting to harm herself. These incidents were not motivated by anger or malice but occurred during acute psychological crises. Neighbors later described her as “begging for help,” “terrified of her own mind,” and “desperate for someone to intervene.”

Despite these warning signs, she did not receive adequate mental‑health treatment. No comprehensive psychiatric evaluation was conducted, and no emergency intervention occurred. At the time of the offense, her mental condition had deteriorated to the point that she was experiencing auditory hallucinations, disorganized thinking, and impaired judgment consistent with severe postpartum psychosis.

These facts demonstrate that the defendant’s actions occurred during a period of profound mental instability, that she repeatedly sought assistance, and that her conduct was driven by untreated psychiatric illness rather than deliberate or calculated intent.

Introduction

This memorandum examines whether the Eighth Amendment permits the imposition of the death penalty when the defendant was experiencing severe postpartum psychosis, repeatedly sought help, reported hearing voices, attempted suicide, and exhibited profound mental instability. These circumstances raise substantial concerns about diminished culpability, the reliability of intent findings, and the constitutional administration of capital punishment.

Issue

Issue: Whether the Eighth Amendment permits the imposition of the death penalty when the defendant was experiencing severe postpartum psychosis, repeatedly sought help, reported hearing voices, attempted suicide, and exhibited profound mental instability that undermines intent, reduces moral culpability, and raises substantial concerns about arbitrariness, reliability, and the constitutional administration of capital punishment.

I. General Constitutionality of Capital Punishment

The Eighth Amendment limits the state’s power to impose capital punishment even though the death penalty is not per se unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has made clear that capital punishment must be administered in a manner that is not cruel, unusual, or arbitrary. The core inquiry is not merely whether the defendant committed a serious crime, but whether the state may impose the most severe penalty under the specific circumstances of the case.

In Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972), the Court held that the death penalty as then administered was unconstitutional because it was imposed in an arbitrary and capricious manner. The decision emphasized that uncontrolled discretion produced inconsistent and unpredictable outcome.

Four years later, Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), upheld revised capital statutes that provided guided discretion, bifurcated proceedings, and appellate review. Gregg stressed that capital sentencing must meaningfully channel discretion and ensure individualized consideration of mitigating evidence.

Applied here, the sentencer’s failure to meaningfully consider the defendant’s severe postpartum depression, psychosis, repeated suicide attempts, and documented pleas for help renders the death sentence unreliable under Gregg. These circumstances demonstrate profound mental instability that required individualized consideration.

II. Severe Mental Illness Reduces Culpability

A defendant’s severe mental illness may significantly weaken the justification for execution because it affects moral blameworthiness, comprehension, and the reliability of the sentencing process. When a defendant’s mental state is seriously impaired, the rationale for the death penalty becomes less persuasive because the sentence no longer reflects the same degree of conscious and deliberate wrongdoing.

In Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that executing individuals with intellectual disability violates the Eighth Amendment because their impairments jeopardize the reliability and fairness of capital proceedings. Although Atkins addressed intellectual disability specifically, its reasoning regarding reduced moral culpability extends by analogy to severe mental illness.

In Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits executing a prisoner who is insane because such execution serves no legitimate penological purpose. Panetti v. Quarterman, 551 U.S. 930 (2007), refined this standard, holding that a prisoner must have a rational understanding of the State’s rationale for execution. In Madison v. Alabama, 139 S. Ct. 718 (2019), extended Panetti by holding that dementia and other disorders—not just psychosis—can render a prisoner incompetent for execution.

Beyond competency at the time of execution, the Court has required that mental illness be considered as a mitigating factor during sentencing. In Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 (1982), the Court held that the sentencer must consider “any relevant mitigating evidence,” including emotional disturbance and mental development. Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), similarly held that the sentencer must be permitted to consider any aspect of the defendant’s character or record as a basis for a sentence less than death.

Applied here, evidence of postpartum depression or psychosis that substantially impaired the defendant’s ability to appreciate the nature or wrongfulness of her conduct reduces her moral culpability under Atkins and requires full mitigating consideration under Eddings and Lockett.

III. Disputed Intent Matters

Capital punishment depends on a reliable finding of the mental state required by law. If evidence of premeditation or specific intent is weak, contested, or inconsistent with the defendant’s mental condition, the constitutional case for execution becomes substantially weaker.

In Eddings, the Court held that the sentencer must give full effect to mitigating evidence, including emotional disturbance. The diminished-capacity doctrine likewise allows defendants to offer evidence of mental abnormality to negate specific intent. Burt Lancaster v. Metrish, 683 F.3d 740 (6th Cir. 2012), explains that mental disease or defect may prevent formation of the specific mental state required for the most serious offenses.

State courts have echoed this principle. In State v. Evans, 203 Conn. 212 (1987), the Connecticut Supreme Court recognized that mental illness may affect criminal liability by diminishing culpability or eliminating responsibility entirely.

Under Lockett, the sentencer must consider any aspect of the defendant’s character or record as a basis for a sentence less than death. Evidence that the defendant was experiencing auditory hallucinations, disorganized thinking, and suicidal impulses undermines the reliability of any finding of premeditation or specific intent. Her repeated pleas for help and disclosures of schizophrenia further weaken the prosecution’s theory of deliberate action.

IV. Racial Disparity and Arbitrariness

Racial disparity in capital sentencing supports the broader argument that the death penalty is vulnerable to arbitrary application. Historical and modern studies have repeatedly shown disparities based on the race of the defendant and the race of the victim.

In McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279 (1987), the Court considered statistical evidence showing significant racial disparities in capital sentencing. Although the Court held that statistical evidence alone was insufficient to prove purposeful discrimination, it acknowledged that the disparities were “deeply troubling” and raised serious concerns about arbitrariness.

When combined with substantial mitigating evidence—such as severe mental illness—the risk of arbitrary imposition strengthens the argument against execution. The defendant’s postpartum psychosis and history of suicide attempts may increase the risk of unpredictable reactions to sedatives used in lethal injection, reinforcing the constitutional concerns identified in Baze, Glossip, and Bucklew.

V. Method of Execution

Even when the death penalty is lawful in principle, the method used to carry it out may violate the Constitution if it creates an unacceptable risk of severe pain.

In Baze v. Rees, 553 U.S. 35 (2008), the Court held that a prisoner must show that the method creates a demonstrated risk of severe pain and that a feasible, readily implemented alternative exists that would significantly reduce that risk. In Glossip v. Gross, 576 U.S. 863 (2015), reaffirmed this standard. In Bucklew v. Precythe, 139 S. Ct. 1112 (2019), confirmed that the Baze/Glossip framework governs all method-of-execution claims, including individualized challenges based on unique medical conditions.

Applied here, the defendant’s postpartum psychosis and history of suicide attempts may increase the risk of unpredictable reactions to sedatives used in lethal injection, reinforcing the constitutional concerns identified in Baze, Glossip, and Bucklew.
