Capital Punishment Constitutional Analysis

AuthorPatricio Lucero, Paralegal
RecipientSupervising Attorney
DateAugust 7, 2026
SubjectEighth Amendment Issues of Law Concerning Mental Illness and Capital Sentencing
Statement of Facts

The defendant is a young mother who, in the months preceding the offense, experienced severe postpartum depression and symptoms consistent with postpartum psychosis. During that period she became increasingly distressed, disorganized, and unable to function normally.

Her mental deterioration escalated into multiple suicide attempts. During these episodes, she caused physical harm to her children while attempting to harm herself. The medical record reflects repeated crisis behavior and escalating instability.

Despite these warning signs, she did not receive adequate mental-health treatment. No comprehensive psychiatric evaluation was conducted, and no emergency intervention occurred before the offense. The lack of timely treatment likely worsened the mental-health crisis.

These facts demonstrate that the defendant’s actions occurred during a period of profound mental instability, that she repeatedly sought assistance, and that her condition was not meaningfully addressed by the system.

Introduction

This memorandum examines whether the Eighth Amendment permits the imposition of the death penalty when the defendant was experiencing severe postpartum psychosis, repeated suicidality, and impaired mental judgment at the time of the offense.

Issue

Issue: Whether the Eighth Amendment permits the imposition of the death penalty when the defendant was experiencing severe postpartum psychosis, repeatedly sought help, and exhibited profound mental instability that substantially undermines moral blameworthiness and sentencing reliability.

I. General Constitutionality of Capital Punishment

The Eighth Amendment limits the state’s power to impose capital punishment even though the death penalty is not per se unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has made clear that the punishment must be applied with fairness, individualized consideration, and procedural reliability.

In Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238 (1972), the Court held that the death penalty as then administered was unconstitutional because it was imposed in an arbitrary and capricious manner. Four years later, Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), upheld revised capital statutes that provided guided discretion, bifurcated proceedings, and appellate review.

II. Severe Mental Illness Reduces Culpability

A defendant’s severe mental illness may significantly weaken the justification for execution because it affects moral blameworthiness, comprehension, and the reliability of punishment. Under the Eighth Amendment, the state cannot ignore substantial mitigating evidence that calls the fairness of death into question.

In Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that executing individuals with intellectual disability violates the Eighth Amendment because their impairments reduce moral culpability. In Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits executing a prisoner who is insane because such execution serves no legitimate penological purpose.

Beyond competency at the time of execution, the Court has required that mental illness be considered as a mitigating factor during sentencing. In Eddings v. Oklahoma, 455 U.S. 104 (1982), the Court emphasized that the sentencer must consider all relevant mitigating evidence, including emotional disturbance and mental illness, before imposing death.

III. Disputed Intent Matters

Capital punishment depends on a reliable finding of the mental state required by law. If evidence of premeditation or specific intent is weak, contested, or inconsistent with severe mental illness, the death sentence becomes more vulnerable to constitutional challenge.

Under Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), the sentencer must consider any aspect of the defendant’s character or record as a basis for a sentence less than death. Evidence that the defendant was in the throes of postpartum psychosis and suicidal crisis is highly relevant to culpability and sentence selection.

IV. Racial Disparity and Arbitrariness

Racial disparity in capital sentencing supports the broader argument that the death penalty is vulnerable to arbitrary application. Historical and modern studies have repeatedly demonstrated unequal treatment in charging, sentencing, and execution decisions.

In McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279 (1987), the Court considered statistical evidence showing significant racial disparities in capital sentencing. Although the Court held that such evidence, standing alone, does not establish unconstitutional purpose, the risk of arbitrary application remains a central concern when combined with profound mental illness and inadequate mitigation review.

V. Method of Execution

Even when the death penalty is lawful in principle, the method used to carry it out may violate the Constitution if it creates an unacceptable risk of severe pain. The method of execution must be evaluated under a standard that accounts for medical realities and the prisoner’s condition.

In Baze v. Rees, 553 U.S. 35 (2008), the Court held that a prisoner must show that the method creates a demonstrated risk of severe pain and that a feasible, readily implemented alternative exists. Applied here, the defendant’s postpartum psychosis and history of suicide attempts may increase the risk of unpredictable medical reactions to sedatives used in lethal injection.

VI. Historical and Moral Background Supports Restraint

Historical traditions show longstanding concern with proportionality, moral discernment, and individualized judgment. Ancient and modern legal traditions alike recognize that punishment should reflect the offender’s actual blameworthiness and mental state.

Conclusion

The Eighth Amendment does not permit the imposition of death without a careful and individualized assessment of mental illness, diminished culpability, and reliable sentencing procedures. The defendant’s severe postpartum psychosis, repeated suicidality, and documented instability all support the conclusion that execution would be constitutionally suspect.
